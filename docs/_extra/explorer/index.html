STalign 2.0 · how the alignment works

Bend one tissue section onto another

Two sections of the same organ never line up exactly. Each is cut, rotated, stretched and torn a little differently. STalign finds a smooth, invertible warp that carries the cells of one section (the source) into the coordinates of another section or an atlas (the target), so the same anatomy lands in the same place.

New to image registration? Read the steps in order and move the controls. Each picture was computed by STalign on the tutorial's MERFISH data.

Before the steps

What goes in, what comes out

In: two sets of cells, each an array of x and y positions in µm. The target can also be a histology image, or a 3D atlas such as the Allen mouse brain (with LDDMM_3D_to_slice).

Out: a transformation, not a list of matched cells. It has two parts: an affine map \(A\) (rotation, scaling, shear, shift) and a smooth deformation \(\varphi\). A source cell at \(x\) goes to

\[ y = A\,\varphi(x). \]

\(\varphi\) is a diffeomorphism: smooth, with a smooth inverse. Tissue may stretch or compress, but it cannot fold over itself or tear. Because the result is a map of space, you can apply it to any points or images measured on the source section: cells, spots, annotations, gene expression images.

# xI, yI: source cells; xJ, yJ: target cells (µm)
from STalign import STalign
import numpy as np

out = STalign.align_cells(xI, yI, xJ, yJ)

# move the source cells into target coordinates
# (points are in row, column order: [y, x])
yx = STalign.transform_points_source_to_target(
    out['xv'], out['v'], out['A'], np.stack([yI, xI], 1))
yx = yx.cpu().numpy()

align_cells runs steps 1 to 4 below with settings chosen for cells. The tutorials also show each step separately with rasterize and LDDMM, which is the way to learn what each setting does.

rasterize

Cells become images

LDDMM compares images, not lists of points, so each set of cells is first turned into a density image. Every cell adds a small Gaussian blob that sums to 1 over the pixels, so a pixel holds the number of cells near it:

\[ I(x_k) = \sum_{i} \frac{\exp\!\big(-\lVert x_k - p_i\rVert^2 / 2s^2\big)}{\sum_{k'} \exp\!\big(-\lVert x_{k'} - p_i\rVert^2 / 2s^2\big)}, \] \[ s = 2\cdot\texttt{blur}\cdot dx, \]

where \(p_i\) are the cell positions, \(x_k\) the pixel centers, \(dx\) the pixel size and blur the kernel width in pixels.

Smaller pixels keep detail but cost time: each LDDMM iteration touches every pixel, so 15 µm pixels take about 4 times the work of 30 µm pixels. Too much blur erases the layers and ventricles that guide the fit. Too little leaves the speckle of individual cells, which two different sections do not share.

Look closer

  • blur is in pixels, so the same blur is twice as wide at twice the pixel size. Compare 15 µm and 60 µm at blur 2. rasterize(..., blur_um=30) sets the width in µm instead.
  • The tutorials use \(dx = 30\) µm and blur=1.5, a 90 µm kernel. align_cells uses 15 µm pixels and a 30 µm kernel and fits coarse to fine: first on images pooled 4×4, then 2×2.

2
prefit='auto'

A rough affine fit first

LDDMM improves an alignment by gradient descent, so it finds the best fit near where it starts. If the source starts upside down, the nearest fit is a wrong one.

So before LDDMM, STalign runs a coarse affine pre-fit. It tries 12 starting rotations, 30° apart, fits only the affine map

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} L & T \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x \mapsto Lx + T \]

from each start on downsampled images, keeps the one with the lowest energy and refines it. In 3D it tries tilted and shifted slice planes instead. prefit=False turns it off, and it is skipped when you give landmarks.

To test it, we rotated the tutorial's source by a known angle and aligned it from there, with and without the pre-fit (1,000 iterations each). Since the rotation is known, a correct fit puts every cell where the fit of the unrotated source puts it. The error is that distance.

Look closer

  • Without the pre-fit, only the unrotated case succeeded (median error under 50 µm). At 5° the median error was already 59 µm, at 15° 325 µm, and from 30° on it was 0.9 to 5.8 mm. With the pre-fit, every angle from 0° to 180° had a median error of 2 to 4 µm.
  • A failed fit can look fine. At 15° and 30° without the pre-fit the overlay is nearly white, because the deformation bent tissue onto the wrong tissue until the outlines matched. Check alignments against anatomy you know, such as the ventricles and the hippocampus.

3
LDDMM

A smooth flow of space

LDDMM does not move pixels one by one. It moves space along a velocity field \(v_t\), a smooth vector field on a grid, and following the flow for unit time gives the map:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\varphi_t(x) = v_t\big(\varphi_t(x)\big), \qquad \varphi_0(x) = x, \qquad \varphi = \varphi_1. \]

STalign takes \(n_t = 3\) time steps. Because each step is a small, smooth move, their composition is smooth and invertible. That is what keeps tissue from folding.

\(A\) and \(v\) are found together by minimizing one energy:

\[ \begin{aligned} E(A, v) = {} & \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sigma_M^2} \sum_{x} W_M(x)\,\Big[ f\big(I\circ\varphi^{-1}\circ A^{-1}\big)(x) - J(x) \Big]^2}_{E_M \text{: the images still differ}} \\ & + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sigma_R^2} \sum_{t} \big\lVert (1 - a^2\Delta)^{p}\, v_t \big\rVert^2}_{E_R \text{: the flow is rough}} \end{aligned} \]

\(I\) is the source image and \(J\) the target. \(I\circ\varphi^{-1}\circ A^{-1}\) is the deformed source, sampled on the target's pixels \(x\). \(f\) is a linear intensity map (an offset and a scale), refitted every iteration so the two images need not share units. \(W_M\) weighs each target pixel (step 4). The operator \((1 - a^2\Delta)^{p}\) penalizes velocity that changes over distances shorter than \(a\) (defaults \(a = 500\) µm, \(p = 2\)).

Each iteration takes one gradient step,

\[ v \leftarrow v - \epsilon_V\, K * \nabla_{v} E, \] \[ L \leftarrow L - \epsilon_L \nabla_L E, \qquad T \leftarrow T - \epsilon_T \nabla_T E, \]

where \(K = (1 - a^2\Delta)^{-2p}\) smooths the gradient. It is applied with FFTs. The step sizes \(\epsilon\) are scaled to the images and capped where gradient descent would become unstable.

The setting that matters most is \(\sigma_R\), the balance between the two terms. Since version 2.0 you choose sigma_disp, the typical displacement in µm that the deformation is expected to have (default 94 µm), and STalign converts it:

\[ \sigma_R = \sigma_{\text{disp}}\,\sqrt{C_d\,\pi}\;a^{d/2}\,\sqrt{n_t}\;\frac{dx}{30\ \mu\text{m}}\;\frac{\sigma_M}{s_J}, \]

with \(C_2 = 12\), \(d\) the dimension and \(s_J\) the intensity scale of the target. The factor \(dx\) is there because \(E_M\) is a sum over pixels and \(E_R\) is not, so the same sigma_disp means the same thing at any pixel size. A sigmaR you pass is used as given.

Look closer

  • Iteration 0 already looks close: it is the end of the affine pre-fit. Everything after it is LDDMM, which adjusts \(A\) and bends the grid.
  • At iteration 50 the matching energy \(E_M\) jumps up. That is not a worse fit: it is when the pixel weights \(W_M\) are first re-estimated. They start at 0.5 and rise toward 1 where the images match, so the same mismatch counts for more.
  • No setting here folds the tissue. Too much flexibility shows up as stretching and compression instead: at sigma_disp=1000 some regions shrink to 0.55 times their area and others grow to 2.4 times, for a small gain in image correlation (0.85 to 0.88). At 3 µm the fit stays almost affine. Image similarity alone cannot choose sigma_disp; the default was chosen on test problems with known, ground-truth deformations.

4
mixture='em'

When the target is missing tissue

Real sections tear, fold or are trimmed differently, so the target may lack tissue that the source has. A fit that tries to match every pixel then squeezes the source to cover only what the target has.

The weights \(W_M\) in \(E_M\) handle this. STalign treats each target pixel as either matching the deformed source or background (optionally also artifact), and re-estimates the probabilities during the fit (an EM algorithm). With mixture='em':

\[ W_M(x) = \frac{\pi_M\,\mathcal{N}\big(J(x) \mid f(AI)(x),\, v_M\big)}{\pi_M\,\mathcal{N}\big(J(x) \mid f(AI)(x),\, v_M\big) + \pi_B\,\mathcal{N}\big(J(x) \mid \mu_B,\, v_B\big)} \]

The proportions \(\pi\) and the variance \(v_M\) of a good match are learned from the residuals, so a pixel that fits far worse than is typical moves to the background and stops pulling on the source.

To test it, we removed the 30% of target cells to the right of x = 6.24 mm and aligned the full source to the cut target.

Look closer

  • With mixture=None (the LDDMM default) the source is squeezed into the remaining tissue: only 1.4% of source cells end up past the cut, where about 30% belong, and cells on the kept tissue are off by 186 µm on average. With 'em', 27% hang over the cut, \(W_M\) falls to 0.42 on average over the cut-away region, and the error on kept tissue drops to 115 µm.
  • With mixture=None the weight image is plain white: \(W_M \approx 1\) everywhere. The older weights use fixed variances that never favor the background at these intensities (0 to 3.5 cells per pixel). That is the result, not a display error.
  • 'em' has a cost. On the intact target it moved cells by 31 µm on average (median 14 µm) relative to the plain fit, and in our tests it made targets of uneven cell density worse. So LDDMM keeps mixture=None, and align_cells uses 'em'.

5
transform_points_source_to_target

Moving the cells

The fit is stored as the affine matrix A, the velocity field v and its grid xv. The image term above uses the backward map: each of the \(n_t\) steps moves a point by \(x \leftarrow x - v_t(x)/n_t\). To send source cells forward, transform_points_source_to_target undoes those steps exactly, solving each one for the point that the backward step would bring back:

\[ p_{t+1} = p_t + \tfrac{1}{n_t}\, v_t(p_{t+1}), \qquad y = A\,p_{n_t}. \]

So cells land exactly where the image fit says they should. Version 1 used a forward Euler step instead, which is not the exact inverse; exact_inverse=False still gives it.

transform_points_target_to_source goes the other way, and transform_image_source_to_target and transform_image_target_to_source warp whole images, for example gene expression rasters or annotation masks.

LDDMM_3D_to_slice does the same between a 3D atlas and a 2D section. There \(A\) is a 3D affine map that also chooses the slice plane, and the pre-fit tries tilted and shifted planes.

Inside the code

The public functions live in STalign/STalign.py, with the same names and arguments as version 1. The work happens in private modules. Every LDDMM fit goes through the same front end and then through one of two engines that compute the same iteration.

STalign.py · _lddmm.py

LDDMM, LDDMM_3D_to_slice, rasterize, transform_*, align_cells. Thin wrappers that check arguments and call the modules below.

↓
_lddmm_common.py

prepare_problem builds the velocity grid and the kernels \(L\), \(K\). resolve_defaults runs the pre-fit and fills in sigmaR, steps and niter (from _prefit.py). run_lddmm picks an engine and runs the iterations in segments of 100.

↓ backend='auto'
_lddmm_cpp.py → _cpp/

C++17 engine via pybind11. CPU, float64. One whole iteration in C++ on a std::thread pool with the GIL released; FFTs by the vendored pocketfft. Built by setup.py when a compiler is available.

_lddmm_torch.py

Pure PyTorch engine for any device and dtype (GPU, float32), and the fallback when the C++ engine is missing. Same iteration, same results up to rounding.

_raster.py

Exact rasterization as tiled matrix products of separable Gaussians (86,000 cells in 31 ms).

_transforms.py

Point and image transforms, the exact inverse, and analyze3Dalign.

_cells.py

align_cells: re-rasterize at 15 µm, then LDDMM coarse to fine.

One iteration

  1. Pull the target's pixel grid back through \(A^{-1}\) and the \(n_t\) backward steps of the flow, \(x \leftarrow x - v_t(x)/n_t\).
  2. Sample the source image there (bilinear): the deformed source \(AI\).
  3. Fit the intensity map \(f\) by weighted least squares and form the residual \(f(AI) - J\).
  4. Compute \(E_M\) and its gradient back through the sampling and the flow. The engines assemble these gradients directly instead of recording an autograd graph.
  5. Smooth the velocity gradient with \(K\) in Fourier space and add the \(E_R\) term.
  6. Step \(L\), \(T\) and \(v\).
  7. Every 5 iterations, update the class means; from iteration 50, also the weights \(W_M\), \(W_A\), \(W_B\) (or the mixture EM, with mixture='em').

Ground rules for changes

Both engines are tested against a plain autograd implementation of the original iteration in tests/reference.py; gradients agree to within 1e-9. legacy_defaults=True reproduces the version 1 defaults bit for bit, and results do not depend on the number of threads. STalign.backend_info() says which engine is in use; backend='torch' forces the fallback.

CHANGES.md lists every change since version 1 with its measured effect, and the tests run on Linux and macOS in CI.

Every number and picture on this page was computed with STalign 2.0.0 (commit b220827, C++ engine, 8 threads) on the MERFISH data of the tutorial merfish-merfish-alignment.ipynb. The source starts from the tutorial's rough rotation. docs/explorer/run_all.sh recomputes them.
